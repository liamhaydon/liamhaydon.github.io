Who Gets Flagged?

We trained a simple model to predict which criminal defendants would end up back in court, using 1.4 million real Wisconsin court cases. Then we audited it: who does it flag, who does it get wrong, and should anyone use it?

The scenario

A risk score for probation

A fictional court office, Northfield County, wants a risk score to decide who gets intensive probation supervision. Its goal is to reduce re-offending without supervising people needlessly, and to treat defendants fairly across race and gender.

The data

Real court records

WCLD is a research dataset of about 1.5 million criminal cases from Wisconsin circuit courts (2000–2018). Our model used sex, age, offense type and prior cases. Race was deliberately left out of the model, so we could test whether that actually makes it fair.

The audit

What we found

01

The model is only modestly better than guessing

Always predicting the most common outcome would be right 57.9% of the time. Our model reached 64.7%, close to the published benchmark for this dataset. That's a real signal, but a weak basis for decisions about someone's liberty.

64.7%Model accuracy
57.9%Guessing baseline
0.68AUC
02

Leaving race out doesn't make the model race-blind

Even without race as an input, Native American defendants were flagged as high-risk far more often than Hispanic defendants, and more often wrongly: their false positive rate was nearly double. Adding neighborhood census data widened the gap in who gets flagged, because those columns act as a proxy for race.

37% vs 22%Flagged high-risk
26% vs 15%Wrongly flagged
03

Looking at race or sex alone hides the biggest gaps

When we crossed race with sex, the differences grew. Native American men were wrongly flagged 33% of the time, compared with 5% for Hispanic women. That gap is more than twice as large as what race-only or sex-only summaries showed. We also checked sample sizes so small groups weren't over-interpreted.

33% vs 5%Wrongly flagged
2.2×Bigger than race/sex alone
04

You can't have every kind of fairness at once

When two groups have different underlying rates, a model can't have equal error rates and equal precision at the same time. We showed this with our own numbers. Equalizing error rates nearly closed the false-positive gap but widened the precision gap, and equalizing precision blew the error gaps wide open.

11.5 → 0.7FPR gap (pts), equal odds
8.7 → 13.2PPV gap (pts)
Under the hood

The Python behind it

Two excerpts from our notebook (lightly trimmed). Click to expand.

01 Training the model 
model = make_pipeline(SimpleImputer(strategy="median"),
                      StandardScaler(),
                      LogisticRegression(max_iter=300))
model.fit(X_train, y_train)

test["score"]  = model.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]
test["y_pred"] = (test["score"] >= THRESHOLD).astype(int)

acc = accuracy_score(test["y_true"], test["y_pred"])
auc = roc_auc_score(test["y_true"], test["score"])
  • The pipeline fills in missing values, puts every feature on the same scale, then fits a logistic regression, a simple and explainable model.
  • predict_proba gives each defendant a risk score from 0 to 1. Anyone at or above the threshold (0.5) is flagged as high-risk.
  • Accuracy checks how often the yes/no call is right, and AUC checks how well the score ranks people overall.
02 Measuring fairness by group 
for key, g in frame.groupby(by_list, observed=True):
    tp = int(((g[y] == 1) & (g[pred] == 1)).sum())   # flagged, did re-offend
    fp = int(((g[y] == 0) & (g[pred] == 1)).sum())   # flagged, did not
    fn = int(((g[y] == 1) & (g[pred] == 0)).sum())   # missed
    tn = int(((g[y] == 0) & (g[pred] == 0)).sum())   # correctly not flagged
    n = tp + fp + fn + tn

    row["selection_rate"] = div(tp + fp, n)
    row["FPR"] = div(fp, fp + tn)
    row["FNR"] = div(fn, tp + fn)
    row["PPV"] = div(tp, tp + fp)
  • For each group (each race, sex, or race × sex), we count the four outcomes: right and wrong flags, and right and wrong non-flags.
  • Selection rate is how often a group gets flagged. FPR is how often people who didn't re-offend get flagged anyway. FNR is how often re-offenders get missed.
  • PPV (precision) is how often a flag is correct. We double-checked every number against the Fairlearn library.
Our recommendation

Don't use it as an automatic rule

If Northfield uses a score at all, we'd prioritize equal error rates across groups, because wrongful flags lead to intrusive supervision. But no fairness metric fixes the deeper problems with the data.

The label is flawed

"Re-offending" really means a new Wisconsin court case, and outcomes are missing nearly twice as often for Black defendants as for white defendants.

The trade-off is real

Choosing one kind of fairness means accepting unequal outcomes on another. That's a policy decision, not a technical one.

Keep humans in charge

Advisory only, open to challenge, monitored by subgroup, and never the only reason someone gets added restrictions.
